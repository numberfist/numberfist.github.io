Chapter 2: Baffler

Cabot Cafe, Science Center, Harvard, AKA Clover Cafe

Cabot Cafe in Harvard’s Science Center was moderately buzzed with activity, amongst the usual bustle of the Science Center.  Half the tables were empty as usual, and the other half of the tables, despite having 4 seats per table, had one student, accompanied by the occasional friend.  Most of them typed on their laptops, scribbled in their notebooks, or munched on their lunch in silence.  Unlike most of the cafe, the back of the room near the library was not sparsely populated, rather, clustered around the 3 tables nearest to the entrance to the library, were a tight group of 6 students, engaged in tense conversation.  Scattered among the tables were stapled copies of the first Math 55a problem set.  Nishant sat on the table nearer to Clover, his pset open as he scribbled intently in his notebook.  One table over, Kimberley, instead of sitting, leaned backwards against the chair that was pushed into the table.  One of the students approached her.

“So for problem 11, I was wondering how you got this step here?” he asked, motioning to his notebook.  Nishant looked up briefly with a cold, neutral expression.

“Sure!  It’s extremely easy, really.” Kimberley responded, her voice carrying a decent amount of smug condescension.  She snatched the notebook from him and began writing.  “To get an injective homomorphism from $F_n$ to $F_2$, just act on each generator of $F_n$ to give a conjugate of the generators in $F_2$.  Then you get the answer immediately, it only took me five minutes.  Let me know if you need any more… help,” she finished with a smirk and an emphasis on the word “help”, before dropping her pen on the table and shoving the notebook back towards the student.

Nishant, still watching, got up from his seat and approached the table with Kimberley, while the other student looked at her blankly, then nodded uncomfortably and reached for his notebook.

“Oh and by the way,” Kimberley continued, louder this time, while pulling the notebook away from the student.  “Did you notice how this is actually related to the fundamental groups of topological spaces?”  She grabbed the pen from the table and began writing again in the student’s notebook.  “If we have a wedge product of topological spaces, then the fundamental group is the free product of the fundamental group of each space.  With higher homotopy groups, it’s the same thing abelianized, and even the cohomology ring conveniently becomes the product of the cohomology of each space.  Pretty straightforward if you ask me,” she declared, handing the notebook back.  “Oh, and if anyone else needs help, you know who to find!” she declared to the room, leaning once more on the chair instead of sitting in it, facing away from her notes on the table.  A few heads turned, with expressions somewhere between confusion and annoyance, and one student rolled their eyes quickly.  They quickly resumed their conversations.

“So, you’ve already finished this week’s pset then?” Nishant asked, as he narrowed his eyes at Kimberly.

“Of course I did!” Kimberley scoffed, eying Nishant’s impassive, yet scrutinizing expression.  “In fact, I completed the first 6 psets in advance.  They were quite easy really, so I saw no harm in doing them.”

“So you must have already solved problem 7?” Nishant pressed.  “The one about the dihedral group $D_4$?  In chemistry, it’s called $C_{4v}$, which by construction matches the symmetry group of the 2D square.  If you’ve worked with interhalogen compounds like $\ce{IF5}$ then the subgroup structure becomes obvious.”  He paused.  “But obviously you already know that… right?” he added sarcastically.

Kimberley rolled her eyes. “You seem to have missed the more elegant approach,” she retorted. “If you consider the fundamental group of the Klein bottle and then attach 2-cells such that the correct generators have order 2 and order 4, you get a visual example automatically, since that’s obviously what you’re looking for, if you understand it.”

“Clearer concrete examples make more sense, if you understand that,” Nishant replied quietly, yet pointedly, and turned away.  Kimberley leaned forward as if she had more to say, but stopped as Nishant’s back was already facing her.

Nishant turned towards his table, and saw Sudhi already sitting on his chair, across from one of his friends. On the floor next to Sudhi was Nishant’s notebook, Sudhi’s laptop was placed where Nishant’s notebook was, and Nishant’s pencil was shoved to the center of the table.  Sudhi and his friend Harry were already active in discussion.

“So in this problem, the kernel of $\varphi$ is the center of $G$, right?” He gestured to his computer screen.

His friend nodded.  Nishant walked towards Sudhi with a cold expression and stopped right in front of his notebook.  “You’re in my seat,” he said icily.

Sudhi smirked, completely ignoring Nishant, and continued talking to his friend as if nothing had happened.  “So for $\varphi$ to be an isomorphism, we want the center to be trivial.”

Nishant bent down, picked up his notebook and planted it firmly on the table next to Sudhi’s laptop, positioning himself so that he was standing directly next to Sudhi, such that he could look directly downwards at him.  “I said you’re in my spot,” Nishant said, his voice lower and firmer now.  Sudhi looked up at Nishant and rolled his eyes, but slowly started backing away as Nishant continued to glare at him unflinchingly.

Sudhi stifled a laugh.  “OK, let’s go,” he told his friend.  With that they both got up out of their chairs, and Sudhi picked up his laptop.  Nishant quickly sat down in his seat, adjusted his notebook and pset in front of him, and shot a glance at Sudhi, who was shuffling his feet while holding his laptop in his hand.  Nishant gave Sudhi a slight nod the moment Sudhi’s eyes met his.  Then he dropped his eyes towards his notebook and continued to write.

The classroom was packed with students, so much so that some of them were standing due to a lack of seats.  Students leaned over their notebooks, scribbling furiously while glancing at the projector, which was filled with definitions, examples and properties of normal subgroups, the kernel of a group homomorphism being one of the examples.  Amidst the tense hum of the classroom, Professor Aubert finished penning the statement of the next theorem with his Apple Pencil.

“Given a group homomorphism $\varphi : G \rightarrow H$, we established that the kernel of $\varphi$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, and that $\varphi$ factors uniquely through the quotient, giving us an injective homomorphism $\overline{\varphi}: G/\text{ker}\varphi \rightarrow H$, which, by construction, has the same image as $\varphi$.  That’s the main statement of the First Isomorphism Theorem, but before I go on, any questions?”

Nishant’s hand went up.  “Yes?” Professor Aubert asked.

“Professor, so is it true that $G/N$ is a group if and only if $N$ is a normal subgroup?”

A few students rolled their eyes.  Luciano, a tall student near the back, leaned back in his chair with a smirk.

“Correct, that’s essentially the definition,” Professor Aubert responded.  “$N$ being a normal subgroup of $G$ is the same thing as giving $G/N$ a well-defined group operation inherited from $G$.”

Nishant nodded silently, and continued to write in his notebook.  Another student, Raghav, raised his hand and flicked his hair.

“Yes?” Professor Aubert asked.  A few heads turned towards Raghav, who was already smirking.

“Doesn’t this same type of statement generalize to an exact functor $\mathscr{F}: \mathcal{A} → \mathcal{B}$ of abelian categories, in which $\text{ker}\mathscr{F}$ is a Serre class $\mathcal{S}$ in $\mathcal{A}$ that respects kernels and cokernels of morphisms in $\mathcal{A}$, and $\mathscr{F}$ factors through a fully faithful functor $\overline{\mathscr{F}}: \mathcal{A}/\mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{B}$ on the quotient category?”

A low groan rippled through the class.  Professor Aubert exhaled.  “Yes, that’s true, but let’s discuss that later.”  Raghav leaned back in his chair, still smirking, as more students rolled their eyes and groaned subtly.  Nishant’s eyes narrowed sharply at Raghav just for a moment before he refocused on the professor.

“OK, if that’s all the questions, we can now give the proof for the First Isomorphism Theorem…”

The camera focuses on the projector screen, which goes out of focus, and when it refocuses, the content is different, showing an overview of symmetric groups, transpositions and subgroups.

“Now note that when we say that a finite group $G$ acts on a finite set $X$, we are identifying each element of $G$ with an automorphism of $X$, which effectively gives us a homomorphism from $G$ to $\text{Aut}(X)$, which for sets is the symmetric group on $|X|$ elements.  This homomorphism thus gives us a permutation representation of $G$.”  He underlined $\varphi: G \rightarrow S_{|X|}$ in his notes.

“Professor?” came a voice.  A few heads turned.  Professor Aubert looked up.  “Yes, Raghav?”

Raghav adjusted himself in his seat.  “If we consider $G$ as a category with one object, can we fully represent its action on $X$ by a functor from this category into sets?  By this logic, the Yoneda embedding even gives us an action by $G$ on itself.”  Raghav leaned back casually again and confidently tapped his notebook with the back of his pen.

Aubert froze mid-gesture.  “…Technically yes, one could frame it that way, however, right now, we want to look at the group action as a homomorphism into the symmetric group.”  He forced a smile and exhaled again.  “OK, moving on, note that a finite group $G$ can act on itself by left multiplication, and this tells us that every finite group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of some symmetric group…”

The camera focuses on the projector screen, which goes out of focus, and when it refocuses, the content is different, showing the generic form of a short exact sequence of groups

$0 \rightarrow N \xrightarrow{i} G \xrightarrow{j} G/N \rightarrow 0.$

“Here’s the basic idea behind a short exact sequence, here, we consider a short exact sequence of groups, which gives us an injective homomorphism $i$ and a surjective homomorphism $j$, in which the kernel of $j$ is $N$.  Intuitively, a short exact sequence allows us to characterize $G$ using $N$ and $G/N$.”

A hand went up in the second row.  Professor Aubert straightened, then nodded.  “Yes, Kimberley?”

Kimberly smirked.  “Can’t we also have long exact sequences too?  If you consider a short exact sequence of cochain complexes, doesn’t that give you a long exact sequence on cohomology?”  She glanced around smugly at everyone else, tilted her chin up and folded her arms as she leaned back in her chair.

Professor Aubert adjusted his glasses.  “Yes, that’s correct, and a classical consequence of exactness, however, we are only considering short exact sequences of groups.”

Kimberley leaned back in her chair and nodded.  Before Professor Aubert could continue, Raghav’s hand shot up.  Professor Aubert hesitated, then relented.  “Yes… Raghav?”

Raghav glanced at Kimberley and rolled his eyes.  “Isn’t Kimberley’s result just a consequence of the snake lemma?  If we consider any delta functor on any abelian category, then don’t we get the same result?  The construction is completely general.”  Raghav sat back confidently.  Kimberley’s smirk faded.

Another faint groan rippled through the classroom as people shifted uncomfortably.  Aubert hesitated again.  “Yes, Raghav, but for our purposes, we’re sticking to short exact sequences of groups.”

As Professor Aubert turned back to his iPad, Raghav and Kimberley exchanged a glance.

“Yes, but that assumes that $A$ is abelian so that you can give $A$ the structure of a $G$-module via conjugation,” Professor Aubert answered.  “But either way, group cohomology is outside the scope of—”
